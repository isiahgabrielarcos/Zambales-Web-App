About Zambales

A Province Between Sea and Mountain

Zambales is a landlocked-meets-coastal province on the western edge of Luzon, Philippines. Bounded by the South China Sea to the west and the Zambales Mountain Range to the east, it occupies a strip of dramatic geography that has shaped its people, culture, and economy for centuries.

The province is made up of 13 municipalities and one component city (Olongapo), with Iba serving as the provincial capital. Zambales sits within Central Luzon (Region III) and covers an area of approximately 3,714 km², with a coastline that stretches over 200 kilometres along the South China Sea.

Geography

Where the Mountains Meet the Sea

The Zambales Mountain Range forms the eastern spine of the province, rising to over 2,000 meters at Mt. Tapulao (High Peak). Fed by monsoon rains, rivers carve westward through volcanic foothills before emptying into the South China Sea.

The coast transitions from the mangrove-fringed shores of the north (Candelaria, Masinloc) to the white-sand coves and surf breaks of the south (San Antonio, San Felipe). The interior, scarred and sculpted by lahar flows, has become one of the most striking natural landscapes in the Philippines.

People

The Zambalenyo Spirit

The people of Zambales, collectively known as Zambalenyos, are a blend of indigenous Aeta communities, the Zambal-speaking coastal peoples, and waves of migration from Ilocos, Pampanga, and beyond. This diversity is reflected in the province's food, festivals, and architecture.

The Aeta of the Zambales Range are among the oldest continuous inhabitants of the Philippine archipelago. Their resilience, particularly after the 1991 eruption, and their deep ecological knowledge make them an irreplaceable part of Zambales' living heritage.

The dry season runs from November to May and is ideal for beach trips, island hopping, and the Mt. Pinatubo trek. Skies are clear, seas are calm, and the famous coves are at their most beautiful. June to October brings the southwest monsoon — expect rain, lush greenery, and fewer tourists (plus lower prices!).

By car: take the NLEX then SCTEX towards Olongapo/Subic. From there, head north along the national highway towards Iba — total drive time is roughly 3-4 hours depending on traffic. By bus: Victory Liner operates regular departures from Cubao, Pasay, and Caloocan. Buses go as far as Iba with stops along the way.

ATMs are available in the larger towns — Olongapo, Iba, and San Antonio have the most options. However, once you're heading to remote coves or boat launches, cash is king. We strongly recommend withdrawing enough before you leave town. Most boat operators, guides, and seaside eateries do not accept credit cards.

The main jump-off point for Anawangin and Nagsasa Cove is Pundaquit Beach in San Antonio. From there, you hire a pumpboat (charter the whole boat or join a group). Boats fit around 10–15 people. The ride to Anawangin takes roughly 30 minutes, and Nagsasa about 45. You can do a same-day trip or camp overnight.

Zambales is generally very safe and welcoming to visitors. Exercise standard travel precautions — keep valuables secure, be aware of your surroundings at night, and always let someone know your itinerary before heading to remote areas. Coast guard and local tourism officers are present at major jump-off points.
