Before You Go

We've compiled the most common questions travellers ask before heading to Zambales. Can't find your answer? Contact us directly.

The dry season runs from November to May and is the ideal time for beach trips, island hopping, and the Mt. Pinatubo trek. Skies are clear, seas are calm, and the famous coves are at their most beautiful. June to October brings the southwest monsoon — expect rain, lush greenery, and fewer tourists (plus lower prices!).

By car: take the NLEX then SCTEX towards Olongapo/Subic. From there, head north along the national highway towards Iba — total drive time is roughly 3–4 hours depending on traffic. By bus: Victory Liner operates regular departures from Cubao, Pasay, and Caloocan. Buses go as far as Iba (the provincial capital) with stops along the way.

ATMs are available in the larger towns — Olongapo, Iba, and San Antonio have the most options. However, once you're heading to remote coves or boat launches, cash is king. We strongly recommend withdrawing enough before you leave town. Most boat operators, guides, cottage rentals, and seaside eateries do not accept credit cards.

The main jump-off point for Anawangin and Nagsasa Cove is Pundaquit Beach in San Antonio. From there, you hire a pumpboat (charter the whole boat or join a group). Boats fit around 10–15 people. The ride to Anawangin takes roughly 30 minutes, and Nagsasa about 45. You can do a same-day trip or camp overnight (bring your own tent). Book your boat in advance during peak season (Holy Week, long weekends).

Yes. All Mt. Pinatubo treks must be arranged through the Mt. Pinatubo Tourism Office in Capas, Tarlac. You'll need to register, pay an environmental and guide fee, and hire a mandatory local guide. 4×4 vehicles are used to cross the lahar plains before the hike begins. Tours are best booked in advance — many operators can arrange permits and transport as a package from Manila or Olongapo.

Zambales is generally very safe and welcoming to visitors. Like anywhere, exercise standard travel precautions — keep valuables secure, be aware of your surroundings at night, and always let someone know your itinerary before heading to remote areas. The local communities are hospitable and tourism is an important livelihood for many families here. Coast guard and local tourism officers are present at major jump-off points for safety.

Yes — overnight camping is allowed and highly recommended! There are no hotels or resorts in either cove, so it's bring-your-own-tent. There are basic cottages and food stalls (for snacks and simple meals) run by locals. There is no electricity in Anawangin and Nagsasa, which makes the stargazing extraordinary. Bring drinking water, a portable charger, and pack out all your trash.

Essentials include: sunscreen (reef-safe if you're snorkelling), a reusable water bottle, insect repellent, a dry bag for boat rides, sandals and trekking shoes if you're doing the Pinatubo hike, a headlamp for camping, and plenty of cash. If you're island hopping, bring snorkel gear (rentals are available but limited). A light rain jacket is useful during the shoulder months.

Yes — they genuinely are! Zambales is famous for producing some of the sweetest mangoes in the Philippines, particularly the Carabao variety. The combination of volcanic soil and the coastal climate creates fruit with a rich, deep sweetness and almost fibre-free flesh. Buy fresh mangoes from roadside stalls along the national highway, or take home dried mango packs as pasalubong. Peak mango season is March to June.
